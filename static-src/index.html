📖 How to Use a Sextant
Anatomy

Simplified side-view schematic — proportions are illustrative, not to scale.

Why it works: double reflection

The body's image (amber path, reflected twice) and the horizon (green, seen straight through the clear half of the horizon mirror) arrive at the eye along the same line of sight — so you see them superimposed, and Hs is read directly off the arc when they touch. Rotating the index mirror by an angle θ shifts the reflected image by 2θ, which is why the arc only spans ~60–70° of actual metal but is graduated to read altitudes up to ~120°.

Taking a sight
  1. Check the index correction (IC) first. Set the micrometer drum to 0°00' and sight the horizon. If the direct and reflected horizon lines don't form one continuous line, turn the drum until they do, then read the arc — if that reading is on the arc (past zero), it's a positive correction; if it's off the arc (before zero), it's negative. Enter that signed value into the calculator's "Index Correction" field each session, since it can drift.
  2. For a Sun sight, swing a shade glass in front of both mirrors before looking through the telescope — never look at the Sun's direct or reflected image unshaded. Stars and planets need no shading.
  3. Sight the horizon directly below where the body will appear, then swing the index arm out along the arc until the body's reflection shows up in the horizon mirror.
  4. Bring the image down to the horizon with the micrometer drum: for the Sun or Moon, land the lower limb (bottom edge) just tangent to the horizon (upper limb if the lower is obscured — set "Limb" accordingly below); for a star or planet, bring its center exactly onto the horizon.
  5. Rock the sextant a few degrees side to side around the sighting axis — the reflected image will swing through a shallow arc. The correct reading is at the bottom of that swing, where the body just grazes the horizon; this confirms the sextant is truly vertical.
  6. Freeze the moment. The instant the body touches the horizon at the bottom of the rock, note the UTC time to the second (GHA moves ~15' per minute) and read Hs — whole degrees off the arc, minutes off the micrometer drum.
  7. Feed it into the sextant calculator — Hs (deg + min), limb, index correction, height of eye, the UTC date/time of the sight, and your DR position.
Star sights
  1. Work in nautical twilight — the ~20–30 minute window (roughly 6–12° sun below the horizon) when the horizon is still lit enough to see clearly but the sky is dark enough for 2nd-magnitude stars to show. Evening twilight starts right after sunset; morning twilight ends right before sunrise. Outside that window you'll have one or the other, not both.
  2. Pre-plan which stars you'll shoot before the sky darkens — once it's dark enough to see faint stars, the horizon is often too dark to sight against. Use a star finder (e.g. Pub. 2102-D) or simply recognize bright, well-known stars/constellations, and note each one's rough compass bearing so you can find it quickly by swinging the sextant to that heading with the arm near 0°.
  3. No shade glasses are needed for stars or planets — swing them clear of the light path entirely.
  4. Bring each star's point of light exactly onto the horizon (there's no limb to choose, unlike the Sun or Moon), rock the sextant to confirm vertical as usual, and log Hs and the UTC time to the second for each one.
  5. Shoot 3+ stars spread roughly 120° apart in azimuth if you can — three well-spaced lines of position cross to give a small "cocked hat" fix instead of just one line, and errors in any single sight are much easier to spot. Work fast: twilight is short and each star's line is only good for the instant you took it.
  6. Feed each sight into the calculator separately — same Hs / limb ("none" for a star) / IC / height-of-eye / UTC time / DR fields as a Sun sight — and plot the resulting intercepts from the same DR position to build the fix.
Sun sights
  1. A single Sun sight gives one line of position (LOP), not a fixed point — it only tells you you're somewhere along a line perpendicular to the Sun's azimuth. Two Sun sights hours apart (e.g. mid-morning and mid-afternoon), advanced to a common time along your DR track, cross to form a running fix.
  2. Use lower limb (bottom edge tangent to the horizon) unless it's obscured by cloud, in which case use upper limb and set "Limb" to upper in the calculator — the semi-diameter correction flips sign accordingly.
  3. For a noon sight (Local Apparent Noon), latitude falls out directly — no sight reduction tables needed. Starting a few minutes before your estimated LAN (roughly when the Sun crosses your meridian, bearing due north or south of you), take repeated sights as the Sun climbs: Hs will keep increasing, pause at its peak, then start to fall. The highest reading, right at the pause, is Ho at meridian passage. Compute zenith distance z = 90° − Ho, and name it opposite the Sun's bearing at that instant (Sun bearing south of you ⇒ name z "north", and vice versa). Then: same name as Dec → Lat = z + Dec; contrary names → Lat = z − Dec, taking the name of whichever of z/Dec is larger.
  4. Log the exact UTC time for a regular (non-noon) Sun line just like a star — GHA moves fast enough (~15'/min) that a 30-second timing error shifts the LOP by about 7' (7nm).
Reducing a sight by hand

This is exactly what the calculator below automates — worth knowing so you can still get a fix with nothing but the Nautical Almanac, Pub. 249 (linked below), and a calculator/pencil if the boat's electronics ever fail.

  1. Sextant altitude (Hs) → Apparent altitude (Ha): apply index correction (sign as observed in the IC check) and subtract dip, since the horizon appears lower than true because you're standing above it: Ha = Hs + IC − Dip. Dip ≈ 0.97'×√(height of eye in feet), or read it straight from the dip table inside the Nautical Almanac's front cover.
  2. Apparent altitude (Ha) → Observed altitude (Ho): apply the altitude corrections for the body: Ho = Ha − Refraction ± Semi-diameter + Parallax. Refraction (always subtracted) comes from a refraction table indexed by Ha — largest near the horizon (~34' at 0°), negligible near the zenith. For the Sun, add semi-diameter for a lower-limb sight or subtract it for upper-limb (~16', tabulated daily in the Almanac since it varies slightly with Earth-Sun distance through the year), plus the small (<0.2') horizontal parallax correction. Stars need neither SD nor parallax — they're effectively point sources at infinite distance.
  3. Look up the body's position: from the Nautical Almanac's daily pages, read GHA and Dec for the Sun (or GHA Aries plus the star's tabulated SHA, for a star) at the sight's UTC hour, then interpolate for the minutes/seconds past the hour using the back-of-book increments table.
  4. GHA → Local Hour Angle (LHA): LHA = GHA + east longitude, or LHA = GHA − west longitude (add 360° first if the result would be negative). LHA is how far the body has traveled west of your meridian.
  5. Look up the computed altitude and azimuth (Hc/Zn) in Pub. 249 using your DR latitude, the body's declination, and LHA as the three table arguments, interpolating for minutes of declination — this is exactly what the sight reduction tables linked below are for, so you don't have to solve the spherical triangle by trigonometry directly (though if you want to: sin Hc = sin(Lat)·sin(Dec) + cos(Lat)·cos(Dec)·cos(LHA), with Zn from the matching four-quadrant azimuth formula).
  6. Compare Ho to Hc — the intercept: Intercept = (Ho − Hc) × 60, in nautical miles (1' of arc = 1nm). If Ho > Hc you're closer to the body than the DR position, so plot the intercept toward Zn from the DR point; if Ho < Hc, plot it away from Zn. Draw the line of position through that plotted point, perpendicular to the Zn bearing — your position is somewhere on it. Cross two or more LOPs (different bodies, or the same body re-shot later and advanced along your track) to get a fix.
